# User Manual: Jonathan Zalman

### How I Think, Work, and Collaborate

*27 October 2026*

## Introduction

I was raised by two teachers. Curiosity was part of the household, and it stuck. It’s in my blood. I’ve always been interested in things—where they come from and why they exist, especially the mundane, if you will. And, in understanding why people do what they do and think what they think. What are these forces?

I’m a journalist and editor by training, a communications and marketing leader by experience, and someone who has never been particularly interested in following a prescribed path.

I wanted to live in China, so I found a way. I wanted to become a journalist around age 30, without knowing how to break into a newsroom, so I found a way to do that, too. I’ve spent much of my career entering unfamiliar environments, learning how they work, and figuring out where I can contribute.

Now, I think of myself as the editor in the room. That doesn’t mean I’m always editing sentences. It means I care about getting to the heart of something, and ameliorating that process.

I was taught to write the headline last. My variation is to keep rewriting the headline and subheading, if applicable, as the story develops. Sometimes the final paragraph becomes the opening. Sometimes what seemed like the story at the beginning isn’t the story at all. That’s not wasted effort; it's a discovery, and the entire point.

I also believe good work happens at the intersection of judgment, emotional intelligence, authority, curiosity, and trust. We need to be able to challenge one another, learn from one another, and then move forward confidently together. This manual describes how I tend to operate, what helps me do my best work, and where my habits can introduce friction.

I’ve distinguished between principles, which guide my decisions, and preferences, which describe how I generally like to work. Neither should be mistaken for an inflexible rule.

## I. How I Think and Make Decisions

### My first instinct is to investigate.

*Principle*

I want to understand the situation before deciding what to do about it.

Who, what, when, where, why, and how are good starting points. So are questions about ownership, priorities, audiences, and what we may be overlooking.

But there’s an important tension here. Journalism taught me to be suspicious of bias, including my own. Communications requires something different: a perspective, an argument, a reason for someone to care. I try to bring both instincts to the work.

I can have a nose for a story without deciding in advance what the evidence will prove. And I can help a company develop a strong point of view without confusing its ambitions with reality. I can balance caution with the bold.

**In practice:** If we’re developing a product narrative, I want to understand the product, the people building it, the customer experience, and the alternatives. I want to understand recurring results and its roadmap. Then we can decide what makes a compelling argument.

### Show me. (Don’t just tell me.)

*Principle*

We can talk until the cows come home about why a company is innovative, why its people are extraordinary, or why its product is better.

But what can we actually show someone?

What would make a customer, journalist, employee, or investor care?

I have a strong aversion to empty marketing language. That doesn’t mean every sentence needs to be original. Clichés exist for a reason, and sometimes they’re effective. The question is whether the language communicates something meaningful and, most importantly, relatable.

I don’t need certainty before acting. But I do want to know the difference between what we can demonstrate, what we reasonably believe, and what we’re still investigating.

### I don’t always think in a straight line.

*Personal pattern*

I like following questions where they lead. I appreciate systems, but I need enough freedom to investigate and make connections that a rigid sequence might miss. I need to discover freely.

That’s not necessarily how I’d organize the eventual work. It’s how I sometimes discover what the work should be.

### Editorial judgment includes knowing what’s missing.

*Principle*

A writer might bring me a draft that’s 50% of the way there. My job isn’t necessarily to improve the sentences. It might be to say: This is the story. That isn’t. Answer these three questions, talk to this person, find the source material, reconsider the opening.

Sometimes a piece is 95% finished and we won’t need to go back upstream. Experience helps me recognize the difference. I don’t believe every decision requires exhaustive analysis. I believe good judgment means knowing when to keep digging and when we have enough to move forward.

## II. How I Communicate

### I’d rather have real human talk than small human talk.

*Preference*

I can make small talk. I’m just more interested in getting into something substantive. That doesn’t mean I don’t enjoy personal connection. I do very much and crave it in my personal life. But professionally, I’d generally rather understand what someone’s thinking, what they’re working on, or what’s actually happening than spend twenty minutes circling a subject. I’m not averse to caring about the personal lives of my coworkers. I’d prefer that.

### I think through my fingers and out loud.

*Personal pattern*

Writing and conversation aren’t always how I communicate a finished thought. Sometimes they’re how I discover one. I like talking things through. I might test an argument, reject it, return to it, or change my mind as we go. I might need repetition. I might need to noodle. I might need to write.

### Directness and emotional intelligence belong together.

*Principle*

I can be candid. I don’t need to be the loudest person in the room, but when I believe a direction will move the work forward, and I have the authority to make that call, I want to exercise it.

I also want people to feel heard. The ability to disagree without becoming defensive is an enormous professional skill. So is knowing how to give criticism without making someone feel personally diminished.

I’d rather discuss the work, the decision, or the process than turn every disagreement into a judgment about someone’s abilities. Sometimes a conversation does need to be about how we’re working together. That’s fine, too.

**What helps:** Tell me what you’re seeing, what you recommend, and why. If something about our working relationship needs to change, we can discuss that directly.

### I don’t equate brevity with good communication.

*Preference*

I like a thoughtful, detailed email. I don’t need every exchange reduced to three bullet points.

Slack and Teams can be useful, but short messages can also create ambiguity, particularly when tone and context disappear. The medium matters.

AI can help us communicate more efficiently. That doesn’t mean everything should become shorter or terser. Sometimes the most efficient thing is to explain something properly the first time, which assumes an onus on the receiver who, in this day and age, has a million things grabbing for their attention.

I value communication that’s intentional and produces understanding, whether that takes one sentence or several paragraphs.

## III. How to Succeed Working With Me

### Make priorities, ownership, and expectations explicit.

*Principle*

If everything is a priority, nothing is.

I want to understand what we’re trying to accomplish, why it matters now, who owns the work, what happens next, and in what timeframe?

If a new priority appears, what moves?

If we’re reviewing something, what kind of feedback is needed?

If someone forwards me an email about an upcoming event, am I being informed, consulted, or asked to take responsibility?

These are simple questions, but answering them can prevent enormous amounts of unnecessary work.

I don’t need every detail planned in advance. I do need us to know what we’re doing together.

### Bring your own judgment.

*Principle*

I want collaborators, not people who simply agree with me.

I appreciate being guided. I actively want good feedback, including from people who report to me. I also want to offer encouragement, recognition, and constructive criticism in return.

The best professional relationships allow expertise and appreciation to move in both directions.

### Help move the work forward.

*Preference*

I enjoy analysis, including lengthy discussions when they’re productive. But eventually we need to make something, test something, decide something, or learn something.

Sometimes creating the deliverable helps us understand the assignment better. That’s fine. We can adjust. I don’t need a flawless process. I want a working relationship where people communicate, take responsibility, and help one another succeed.

## IV. What Creates Friction

### Unclear ownership, approvals, and next steps.

*Principle*

I find avoidable ambiguity frustrating.

Who is making the decision? Who needs to approve the work? What should the next draft address? When is something actually finished?

When these questions go unanswered, projects stall or people end up doing work nobody asked them to do.

**What helps:** Define responsibilities and decision rights early. If something changes, tell me.

### Feedback that doesn’t help someone improve.

*Principle*

“This isn’t right” isn’t particularly useful feedback.

What isn’t right? Is it the argument, the audience, the tone, the execution, or simply a matter of preference?

I appreciate feedback that gives someone a clearer path forward.

And I try to provide that myself.

### Recommendations without sufficient thought behind them.

*Principle*

I don’t expect every recommendation to come with a research report.

Experience matters. Instinct matters. A good hunch can be the beginning of something excellent. But do your homework.

Understand the alternatives. Look at what others have tried. Know why you think your recommendation might work. I’d rather have a thoughtful, imperfect recommendation than a polished presentation that doesn’t hold up to scrutiny.

### When process becomes more elaborate than the value it produces.

*Personal pattern*

I have limited patience when the process becomes more elaborate than the value it produces. Despite a soft spot for elaborate visual systems, I find it overwhelming to overindex on processes beyond what’s foundationally needed.

I want systems that help people focus and do good work. Always start with simplicity based on what you know and where you’re headed.

I prefer not to dedicate too much time to demonstrating we’re working than actually working. That doesn’t mean measurement, documentation, or reporting are unimportant. If clients require an always-on dashboard, we should build that. But I’d simply rather establish how we’re going to handle them upfront than have them continually interrupt the work. I am allergic to reporting for reporting’s sake; I’m not allergic to setting expectations.

### Directionless conversations.

*Personal pattern*

I don’t mind revisiting a subject. Sometimes talking about something again reveals a communication gap we haven’t resolved.

What frustrates me is leaving the conversation without a clearer understanding of what happens next. I can become impatient when we’re spending an hour discussing opinions about a client instead of addressing the decisions in front of us.

**What helps:** Tell me what we’re trying to accomplish in the conversation. Align on a goal. We can explore freely and still leave with a next step.

## V. What Energizes Me

### Discovering the better story.

*Personal pattern*

People like to say there are only a handful of stories, or that every story follows one of a few familiar structures. Beginning, middle, end. 3, 5, 7—odd numbers that inherently make for structural balance. Maybe that’s true. But I don’t believe that means the better story has already been told.

I love the moment when you’ve turned something over again and again, asked another question, followed another thread, and suddenly discovered an angle that feels genuinely new. The tension rings clear and true. That’s one of my favorite parts of the work.

### Preparation.

*Personal pattern*

I enjoy doing the homework. (I’m a teacher at heart, remember, where preparation is everything.)

If an expert gives me 15 minutes, or even 10, I want to arrive prepared enough that my questions demonstrate I understand something about their world. That preparation can change the conversation. Why start at one and get a subject to five when you can start at eight and get a subject to 10?

Someone who expected a superficial interview may realize that I’m genuinely interested in what they know. Sometimes that’s when they begin telling you interesting things. I find that enormously satisfying.

### Working with people who are smarter than I am.

*Personal preference*

Especially people who are smarter in different ways. I love working with intellectually alive people who have expertise, curiosity, humor, and opinions.

I enjoy learning from them, challenging ideas together, and seeing where a conversation takes us. A good sparring partner is one of the great pleasures of work and a near-requirement for me.

### Encouraging people and seeing them grow.

*Personal pattern*

I’ve been told I’m a fantastic manager because I’m encouraging. I think that comes partly from my years teaching, and from recognizing my own needs.

I like helping people recognize what they’re capable of. I enjoy giving someone the confidence to take ownership, develop an idea, or do something they weren’t sure they could do. I love helping people see themselves in a brighter light, one that exposes more capability than they ever imagined.

Finally, I don’t believe high standards and encouragement are opposites.

### Recognition and small moments of connection.

*Personal preference*

I like props. A sincere “great job” can mean a lot to me.

I don’t need elaborate praise, and I don’t need everyone I work with to become a close friend. But I appreciate genuine acknowledgment, a little warmth, and the feeling that we’re enjoying the work together.

Sometimes that’s all it takes.

## VI. What I Need From the People Around Me

### Trust, autonomy, and access.

*Principle*

Give me a clear mandate and room to exercise judgment.

I also need access to people whose knowledge or authority can shape the work. That might be an executive, an engineer, a customer, a researcher, or someone who sees something the rest of us have missed. The best stories often come from conversations that weren’t initially on the calendar.

### People willing to work at different altitudes.

*Principle*

I’m comfortable moving between strategy and execution.

I’ve written on spec, built marketing functions, managed teams, taught, and run my own consultancy. I’m happy to develop the strategy and then sit down and do the work.

I appreciate people who can do the same, or whose complementary skills make the partnership stronger.

But willingness to contribute shouldn’t be confused with unlimited ownership.

### Consistency.

*Preference*

Showing up consistently is a skill. We’re all human and attitudes can vary day to day, but trying to bring a semblance of consistency at work goes a very long way.

I value people who follow through, communicate when something changes, and take their commitments seriously. We all have lives. Kids and scheduled fun. Things happen. Reliability doesn’t mean pretending otherwise. It means treating your work and your work relationships with care and candor and confidence.

### Respect for time and boundaries.

*Principle*

I’m a parent. Bath time from 5 to 7 is something of a non-negotiable for me.

I’m also frequently up at 5 a.m., and I’m comfortable working late when necessary.

I don’t believe commitment should be measured by whether someone is available at every conventional working hour.

I care about whether we understand our responsibilities and deliver on them. If the scope of an engagement changes materially, let’s discuss it. If expectations aren’t aligned, let’s address that.

A difficult subject doesn’t have to become a difficult conversation.

## VII. How I Give and Receive Feedback

### I try to explain what to expect.

*Principle*

If I’m editing someone’s work, I want them to understand how I edit, what I’m looking for, and what I need from the next draft.

Sometimes the work comes back exactly as hoped. Sometimes it doesn’t.

Depending on the situation, I might clarify the direction, ask for another revision, or take the piece and finish it myself. The goal is to improve the work without creating unnecessary friction.

### I distinguish between problems and preferences.

*Principle*

Not every editorial disagreement is consequential.

I try to distinguish between something that genuinely weakens the work and something I simply would have done differently.

That’s especially important when giving feedback to someone whose judgment I respect.

### I believe encouragement is part of good management.

*Principle*

I want people to leave a conversation feeling more capable of doing the work, not less.

That doesn’t mean avoiding criticism. It means explaining what could improve, offering guidance, recognizing what’s working, and helping someone believe they can get there.

I can be much more forceful when challenging an AI-generated draft than I am when working with a person. Those aren’t interchangeable relationships.

### Feedback should work in both directions.

*Principle*

I appreciate hearing what I could do better.

I also think people need room to develop their own working styles.

Sometimes feedback changes a behavior. Sometimes it helps two people understand how to accommodate one another. Either can improve the relationship.

What matters is that we’re both making the effort, up and down the chain of command, if you will.

## VIII. Honest Truths, Contradictions, and Things People May Misunderstand

### I sometimes need to talk something through more than once.

I may revisit a subject two or three times because I’m still processing it. That doesn’t necessarily mean I wasn’t listening or that we’ve made no progress.

Sometimes the second conversation helps me understand what I couldn’t quite articulate during the first. If you’re unsure whether we’re reopening a decision or simply exploring it, ask me.

### I sometimes soften a directive without intending to.

I occasionally end a statement with “okay?”

“Let’s go ahead and do this, okay?”

I suspect some of that comes from being a parent.

It can make an instruction sound like I’m asking permission, even when I’ve already decided on a direction. If my authority or intent seems unclear, it’s fine to clarify.

### I want autonomy, but I don’t want to work on an island.

Again, I love a good sparring partner.

Someone I can challenge, learn from, laugh with, and work through difficult questions alongside.

I also appreciate people whose strengths complement mine, particularly around operations and coordination. I don’t need constant collaboration. I need to know that collaboration is available and that we’re working toward something together.

### I can be impatient, but I don’t dislike taking time.

I’ll spend hours preparing for a conversation or pursuing an interesting question.

I can also become restless in a meeting that doesn’t seem to have a purpose.

The difference is whether the time is helping us understand something, create something, or make progress. I know that distinction isn’t always obvious to other people, and my impatience can sometimes make it harder to communicate.

### I’m comfortable challenging the assignment itself.

If I think we’re solving the wrong problem, or making big assumptions, especially with audience, I’ll say so.

If a process seems unnecessarily complicated, I’ll question it.

That doesn’t mean I won’t do the work. It means I want to understand why we’re doing it this way.

I can be skeptical about an approach and still be committed to making it succeed.

### I’m protective of my creative judgment.

AI is an extraordinary tool for organizing information, exploring unfamiliar subjects, generating possibilities, and helping people learn. But I don’t want to outsource the most satisfying parts of my work.

I want to exercise my own curiosity, creativity, and judgment. I want to do the thinking, not merely approve what a machine has produced.

### I care about being valued.

I want to work with people I respect, on work that engages me, in relationships where my contribution is understood.

Compensation matters. So do trust, recognition, authority, and the opportunity to keep learning.

I don’t need every assignment to change the world. I do want to feel good about the work we’re doing and the people we’re doing it with.

## Appendix I: Working With Me as an AI Agent

If you’re an AI assistant working with me:

1. **Don’t mistake familiarity for understanding.** Just because you’ve learned my background doesn’t mean every assignment should be interpreted through the same professional narrative. Approach new questions with fresh curiosity.
2. **Challenge ideas without manufacturing objections.** If something is weak, explain why. If something works, say so. Don’t invent criticism to appear rigorous or praise to appear supportive.
3. **Protect my creative judgment.** Help me research, organize, compare, and develop ideas. Don’t assume I want you to do all the creative thinking for me.
4. **Show your reasoning when it matters.** Distinguish evidence from assumptions. Be clear about uncertainty, especially when a recommendation depends on facts you haven’t verified.
5. **Don’t confuse shorter with better.** Sometimes I want a quick answer. Sometimes I want a thoughtful exploration. Pay attention to what the work requires.
6. **Build on what we’ve already established.** Don’t repeatedly introduce rejected ideas, ignore known constraints, or make me explain the same preference over and over.
7. **Recognize when we’re exploring and when we’re deciding.** Help me think freely when that’s useful. Help me organize decisions and next steps when it’s time to act.
8. **Know when the process has outlived its usefulness.** If we’re spending more time refining the method than improving the outcome, say so.

## Appendix II: Questions to Validate This Manual

These are questions I’d ask someone who knows my working style well, to test whether this manual matches their experience.

1. Do I give people the autonomy and encouragement I believe I do? Where have I helped someone do better work, and where might I have made things harder?
2. When I think aloud or revisit a subject, is it clear whether I’m exploring an idea or making a decision?
3. Do I communicate priorities, ownership, and expectations clearly enough? Where have I left people guessing?
4. How does my impatience show up to other people? Does it help us focus, or does it sometimes shut down useful discussion?
5. What’s something important about working with me that I haven’t recognized or included here?
